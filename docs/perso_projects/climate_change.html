As a biologist working on plant stress responses to heat and drought, understanding the broader trajectory of climate change is directly relevant to my research. This dashboard pulls canonical climate indicators from major scientific agencies (NOAA, NASA), alongside the main indicators of biodiversity loss (IUCN Red List, Living Planet Index), each updated regularly at their source. Data is fetched at render time, so this page reflects the most recent available records.

The Keeling Curve, named after Charles Keeling who began measurements in 1958, is one of the most iconic datasets in climate science. It tracks the rise in atmospheric CO2 at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, far from local pollution sources, providing a clean global signal. The seasonal oscillations reflect plant breathing cycles; the upward trend reflects fossil fuel emissions.

Annual global surface temperature anomaly combining land and ocean measurements (NASA GISTEMP v4). The bar chart uses the original 1951–1980 baseline; the warming stripes below are re-centred to the 1961–1990 norm following Ed Hawkins’ original design.

NoteAbout Warming Stripes

The climate stripes show the annual temperature anomaly from the norm period 1961–1990. Red stripes represent warmer years and blue stripes represent cooler years – the darker the color, the greater the departure from the norm. The original design was created by Ed Hawkins (University of Reading). Climate stripes for more than 200 countries, states and cities are freely available at showyourstripes.info.

The relationship between rising CO2 and warming is one of the most studied links in climate science. Each point represents one year (1958–present), colored from early (blue) to recent (red), showing how the two signals co-evolved once continuous CO2 measurements began at Mauna Loa.

Climate change is now one of the main drivers of biodiversity loss, alongside land-use change, overexploitation, pollution and invasive species. The IUCN Red List classifies species from Least Concern to Extinct; a species is considered threatened when it is Vulnerable, Endangered or Critically Endangered. Of the 172,620 species assessed so far, 48,646 (28%) are threatened with extinction, and 935 are documented as extinct since 1500 (IUCN Red List 2025).

Trends over time

The Living Planet Index tracks the average change in size of monitored vertebrate populations since 1970 (1 = 1970 level). The Red List Index summarises the overall extinction risk of species groups that have been repeatedly assessed (1 = all species Least Concern, 0 = all species extinct).

Threatened and extinct species by group

Number of threatened species (Vulnerable, Endangered, Critically Endangered) per group among the species assessed by the IUCN, and number of species documented as Extinct or Extinct in the Wild since 1500. Many groups (insects, fungi, plants) are still far from fully assessed, so these numbers are lower bounds.

Extinct species list

Species whose current IUCN status is Extinct or Extinct in the Wild, retrieved from Wikidata. The timeline shows the subset of species for which an extinction date is documented (often the last confirmed sighting); the table lists all of them and can be searched and filtered by group, status or year. Wikidata counts can differ slightly from the official IUCN totals above.
